Understanding dog leash reactivity starts with knowing what drives the behaviour. Thresholds, distance and counterconditioning. This guide covers the root causes, practical steps and products that help.

## Understanding the Problem

> **Note:** Avoid dominance/restriction-only advice. Escalate bite risk.

Leash reactivity — barking, lunging and straining toward other dogs on walks — is one of the most misunderstood dog behaviours. Most owners believe their dog is aggressive. In the majority of cases, the dog is frustrated or frightened, not dangerous.

**The barrier frustration model**
Dogs who greet freely off-leash often become reactive on-leash because the leash prevents their normal greeting behaviour. They can't approach, sniff and move on. Frustration builds. Barking and lunging become the outlet. These dogs typically do fine off-leash with other dogs — the leash itself is the trigger.

**The fear model**
Dogs who are genuinely uncomfortable around other dogs use reactivity as a distance-increasing strategy. Barking and lunging says "go away." If it works — the other dog retreats, the owner crosses the street — the behaviour is reinforced. Over time it becomes the dog's first response rather than a last resort.

**Threshold is everything**
Every reactive dog has a threshold distance — the point at which a trigger (usually another dog) becomes overwhelming. Below threshold, the dog can still take treats, respond to cues and disengage. Above threshold, the dog is in a reactive state and learning is impossible. Effective training happens just below threshold. Most owners unknowingly train above it.

**Why it gets worse without intervention**
Each reactive episode rehearses the behaviour and slightly lowers the threshold next time. Dogs that react daily become reactive more quickly and at greater distances over time. Intervention while reactivity is mild is significantly easier than managing an established pattern.

## What You'll Need

- **High-value treats**: cheese, cooked chicken, hot dog pieces — something your dog doesn't get elsewhere
- **Treat pouch**: hands-free access matters during walks; fumbling in a pocket costs you the training moment
- **Well-fitted harness or padded collar**: avoid anything that tightens under pressure — punishment-based equipment teaches nothing and can worsen fear
- **Double-handle traffic leash**: the second handle near the collar gives you control in close-pass situations without needing to shorten the leash mid-walk
- **A clear training environment**: start in a place where you can control distance — a park where you can see other dogs from far away, not a narrow footpath

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Foundation

Before working on reactivity directly, establish three things.

**Mark and reward calmness around dogs at distance.** Find a spot where dogs pass at 30–50m. When your dog notices another dog but doesn't react, mark ("yes!") immediately and deliver a treat. You are building the association: other dog appears → I look at it → good things happen. Do this 10–15 minutes per session, 3–4 sessions before moving on.

**Find your dog's threshold distance.** Note the distance at which your dog first tenses up but hasn't yet reacted. Your training zone is just outside this distance — close enough to notice the trigger, far enough to stay calm. Write it down. It will change as you progress.

**Establish a "find it" cue.** Toss treats on the ground and say "find it." Practice this at home first. On walks, "find it" becomes your reset tool — sniffing resets the nervous system and re-engages the brain.

### Step 2: Training Protocol

The core technique is **look at that (LAT) + counterconditioning**.

1. Position yourself at your dog's threshold distance with another dog visible.
2. When your dog looks at the other dog, immediately say "yes" and deliver a treat.
3. Repeat until your dog looks at the other dog and then looks back at you — they're beginning to expect the treat and checking in for it.
4. Gradually decrease the distance over multiple sessions — a few metres at a time, only when the dog is consistently comfortable at the current distance.

**The rule of three:** If your dog goes over threshold three times in one session, end the session. You've pushed too fast or the environment was too unpredictable. Go back to a greater distance next time.

**Emergency U-turn:** When an unexpected dog appears too close, turn calmly and walk the other direction before your dog reaches threshold. Say "let's go" in a cheerful voice. This is management, not training — but it prevents reactive rehearsal.

### Step 3: Proofing and Generalisation

Dogs don't automatically generalise. A dog that stays calm at the park may still react on a narrower street. Proof the skill across:

- Different environments (park, street, car park)
- Different triggers (dogs of different sizes, on- and off-leash dogs, dogs that bark)
- Different conditions (early morning vs. busy afternoon)

At each new environment, restart at a greater distance and work back to your current level. Expect regression — it's normal and doesn't mean you've lost progress. It means the dog needs time to transfer the learning.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

**1. Shortening the leash when a dog appears**
This is the most universal mistake. Tension on the leash signals danger and physically restricts the dog. It directly escalates reactivity. Keep the leash loose — use distance, not tension, to manage proximity.

**2. Training above threshold**
If your dog is barking, they cannot take in new information. Every session above threshold reinforces reactivity. If you're not finding calm moments to reward, you're too close.

**3. Correcting the bark**
A correction (leash pop, noise, verbal reprimand) suppresses the bark but not the underlying emotion. The dog becomes reactive but stops warning. This is more dangerous, not less. Address the emotion, not the symptom.

**4. Inconsistency between handlers**
If one person applies the protocol consistently and another lets the dog rehearse reactivity, progress stalls. Everyone who walks the dog needs to follow the same rules.

**5. Expecting a straight line**
Reactivity training is not linear. Stress, illness, seasons, new environments all cause temporary regression. A bad week doesn't undo months of progress.

## When to Get Professional Help

Seek a qualified behaviourist (IAABC or APDT certified) if:
- Your dog has bitten, broken skin, or made contact during a reactive episode
- Reactivity began suddenly in an adult dog that was previously calm (rule out pain/illness first)
- Your dog redirects aggression onto you when reacting — spinning, biting the leash, turning on the handler
- You've applied the protocol consistently for 8+ weeks with no measurable change in threshold distance
- The reactivity is interfering significantly with the dog's quality of life or your ability to exercise them

A behaviourist will observe the dog, assess whether the root cause is frustration or fear, and tailor a programme accordingly. In some cases medication (SSRIs, situational anxiolytics) combined with training produces faster results than training alone — this is a vet conversation, not a failure.

## Key Takeaways

- Leash reactivity is usually frustration or fear, not aggression — the distinction matters for how you respond.
- Every dog has a threshold distance. Effective training happens just below it; anything above it is not training, it's rehearsal.
- The core technique is counterconditioning: other dog appears → treat appears, consistently, below threshold.
- Never shorten the leash as a dog approaches — tension escalates reactivity every time.
- Seek a certified behaviourist if there's any bite history, handler redirection, or 8 weeks of consistent work with no progress.
