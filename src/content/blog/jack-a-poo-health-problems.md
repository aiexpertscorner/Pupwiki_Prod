## About Jack-A-Poos
Jack-A-Poo is a designer cross between the Russell Terrier and the Poodle (Miniature). This mix usually weighs about 13-26 pounds, often has a curly, low-shed-leaning coat, and often blends an active, people-oriented temperament from both sides of the pedigree. Common monitoring areas include Lens luxation, Patellar luxation.

### What to know about Jack-A-Poo health planning
Jack-A-Poos have 2 documented genetic health conditions: **Lens luxation, Patellar luxation**. Knowing these in advance helps you choose the right insurance timing and ask targeted screening questions at vet appointments. Toy and small breeds like the Jack-A-Poo often live longer but are more prone to dental disease, luxating patellas, and tracheal collapse. Annual dental checks from age 2 onward are more important for small breeds than most owners realise. For any breed with documented genetic conditions, consider enrolling in pet insurance before symptoms appear — most policies exclude pre-existing conditions. With a typical Jack-A-Poo life expectancy of 11–16 years, insurance decisions made in the first year can significantly affect lifetime vet coverage.
### Frequently asked questions about Jack-A-Poo health
**What are the most common health problems in Jack-A-Poos?**
Jack-A-Poos have 2 documented genetic health conditions: Lens luxation, Patellar luxation. These are breed-level statistical risks — not every Jack-A-Poo will develop these conditions. Your vet can recommend targeted screening based on your dog's individual history.
**How long do Jack-A-Poos live?**

**Should I get pet insurance for a Jack-A-Poo?**
With 2 documented genetic conditions, pet insurance can help manage financial risk for Jack-A-Poos. Get a quote before your puppy's first vet visit — some conditions can be flagged as pre-existing quickly.
### Care tips for Jack-A-Poo owners
- Needs 60-90 minutes of purposeful daily exercise plus mental work to stay balanced.
- Coat can mat quickly—brush several times per week and plan regular professional grooming.
- Usually responds well to positive-reinforcement training and early socialization.
